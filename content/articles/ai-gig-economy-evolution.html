The AI Gig Economy: From Microtasks to Institutional Infrastructure

Artificial intelligence is quietly turning “gig work” into a sprawling ecosystem of contract roles that train, test, and govern models across everything from law and medicine to creative writing and safety. Over the last two to three years, data labeling and AI training have shifted from low-skill microtasks to structured, contract roles that rely on deep subject-matter expertise and nuanced human judgment. Companies like Scale AI now coordinate hundreds of thousands of specialized contractors through platforms such as Remotasks and Outlier, pairing automated pre-labeling with human experts to fine-tune frontier models and resolve edge cases.

Surging Demand and Adoption

The broader independent workforce has grown to around 72.9 million U.S. workers in 2025, with freelance and contract arrangements increasingly normalized as a mainstream labor pattern. Within that pool, demand for explicitly “AI-tagged” skills on platforms like Upwork rose by about 109% year-over-year. Furthermore, AI video generation and editing categories saw earnings growth above 300%, signaling that AI training and tooling work is now a central part of the gig economy.

Recent reports show that freelancers are adopting AI tools faster than traditional employees; surveys of global freelancers suggest that around 73–75% have incorporated AI into their workflows, often for research, drafting, and translation tasks.

Labor Pressures and Disruption

Market-level studies find that AI is radically reshaping contract demand. AI-exposed freelance categories like writing and simple coding have seen job posts decline by around 21%, with some text-heavy roles showing drops of roughly one-third since ChatGPT’s release. A Brookings analysis notes that freelancers in occupations most exposed to generative AI—such as copyediting and proofreading—experienced roughly a 2% decline in new monthly contracts and around a 5% drop in monthly earnings.

The Boom in Data Labor

At the same time, gig work tied directly to AI development is scaling rapidly. The World Bank estimates that between 150 and 430 million “data laborers” globally now perform the labeling, tagging, transcription, and moderation work that underpins AI and social media systems. Additionally, a 2026 market study projects the AI red-teaming services sector to grow from roughly USD 1.3 billion in 2025 to over USD 18 billion by 2035.
